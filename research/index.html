Research Interests and Vision

Cells in a developing embryo share the same genome, yet acquire distinct identities and functions. My research seeks to understand how cell fate is established and maintained, and how three-dimensional genome organization and epigenomic regulation control gene expression during development. The epigenetic landscape illustrates how 3D genome organization, the epigenome, and signaling together shape cell-fate decisions.

Mammalian germline development provides a powerful experimental system to address these questions. Germ cells undergo extensive, developmentally programmed reorganization of the genome and epigenome, and physiologically faithful in vitro reconstitution systems make these transitions accessible to mechanistic studies. My previous work has revealed dynamic changes in chromatin organization during germ-cell development and identified molecular mechanisms underlying the unique 3D genome organization of spermatogonia.

Building on these findings, my future independent research program will focus on uncovering quantitative principles of enhancer–promoter regulation and using them to predict and ultimately control gene expression and cell-fate decisions through cis-regulatory elements.

To address these questions, I combine:

  • Ultra-high-resolution 3D genomics, including Micro-C and Region Capture Micro-C (RCMC).
  • Ultra-low-input epigenomics, including carrier-DNA-assisted ChIP-seq (CATCH-seq), to profile chromatin states in rare cell populations.
  • Quantitative modeling, to connect enhancer activity, chromatin contacts, and transcriptional output.
  • In vitro germline reconstitution systems, providing physiologically relevant models of mammalian germ-cell differentiation.

Over the longer term, I aim to extend these principles beyond the germline to other developmental and disease-relevant systems, with the goal of predicting and ultimately engineering cell fate and function. This work will provide a foundation for rational control of cell states, with potential applications in reproductive medicine, regenerative medicine, and disease treatment.

Selected Research Contributions

  1. Nucleome programming is required for the foundation of totipotency in mammalian germline development

    Nagano, M.*, Hu, B.*, Yokobayashi, S., Yamamura, A., Umemura, F., Coradin, M., Ohta, H., Yabuta, Y., Ishikura, Y., Okamoto, I., et al. (2022). The EMBO Journal 41, e110600.

    Germ cells are unique in engendering totipotency, yet the mechanisms underlying this capacity remained elusive because key stages of germ-cell development occur during embryonic development and germ cells are scarce in vivo. To overcome this limitation, we leveraged scalable in vitro germline reconstitution systems that recapitulate mouse germ-cell development and can generate functional gametes capable of producing healthy offspring (Saitou and Hayashi, Science, 2021). Using these systems, I performed extensive 3D epigenomic profiling across development, including ChIP-seq for 13 targets, ATAC-seq, Hi-C, and histone mass spectrometry, integrated with transcriptomic and DNA-methylome datasets. We found an unexpected, progressive increase in euchromatin, accompanied by loss of lamina-associated domains and radial chromosomal repositioning, despite dynamic DNA demethylation and remethylation. We also found increased chromatin insulation during epigenetic reprogramming despite widespread chromatin opening, followed by extensive “insulation erasure” during spermatogonial development. Functionally, incomplete nucleome maturation was associated with impaired spermatogenic potential. Together, our findings defined principles of nucleome programming that create gametogenic progenitors and establish the foundation for nuclear totipotency, and provided the conceptual and experimental basis for my subsequent discovery of mitotic STAG3-cohesin and my future independent program on regulatory control of cell fate.

    PaperDOI: 10.15252/embj.2022110600

  2. Cooperative CTCF-nucleosome oligomerization stabilizes chromatin loop anchors

    Osorio Valeriano, M.*, Stone, A.C.*, Nagano, M.*, Su, B.*, Caccianini, L.*, Hansen, A.S., Farnung, L., and Vos, S.M. (2026). bioRxiv.

    CTCF, together with loop-extruding cohesin, organizes chromatin into loops and topologically associating domains (TADs), two canonical structures of the 3D genome. However, how CTCF-bound chromatin stabilizes long-range genome interactions remained unclear. This study combined structural and biochemical analyses with cellular and genomic experiments to determine how CTCF–nucleosome assemblies and CTCF dimerization stabilize chromatin loops. I led the cellular and genomic work, including the generation of genome-edited mouse embryonic stem cells, Micro-C, quantitative ChIP-seq, and germ-cell differentiation assays. Mutations in the CTCF dimerization interface strongly weakened CTCF–cohesin loops, and quantitative analyses showed that altered CTCF and cohesin occupancy could not explain most of this defect. Read-level Micro-C further revealed nucleosome-interaction patterns consistent with the structural model. Germline reconstitution showed that epiblast-like differentiation was largely preserved whereas subsequent germ-cell induction was severely impaired, connecting the molecular mechanism to developmental function. Together, these findings showed that local CTCF–nucleosome assemblies and CTCF dimerization stabilize chromatin loops. This work demonstrated how ultra-high-resolution 3D genomics can connect molecular structure to genome-wide organization, while physiological germline reconstitution can connect molecular mechanisms to developmental function. This combined expertise now underpins my ongoing work on quantitative enhancer regulation and provides a foundation for my future independent program.

    PreprintDOI: 10.64898/2026.02.06.704447

  3. The mitotic STAG3–cohesin complex shapes male germline nucleome

    Nagano, M.*†, Hu, B.*, et al., and Saitou, M.† (2025). Nature Structural & Molecular Biology 32, 2203–2218.

    Why spermatogonia display unusually weak chromatin insulation was unresolved. Building on my doctoral discovery of insulation erasure, I investigated its molecular basis across the Saitou and Hansen laboratories. As co-first and co-corresponding author, I identified STAG3 as a component of a distinct mitotic STAG3-RAD21 cohesin complex. We showed that its shorter chromatin residence weakens insulation and rewires enhancer-promoter and Polycomb-associated interactions, providing a mechanism for the distinctive 3D genome organization of spermatogonia. STAG3 loss impaired differentiation priming of spermatogonial stem cells both in vitro and in vivo, connecting 3D genome organization to developmental cell-state regulation. This unexpected role of STAG3 in mitotically dividing cells led me to analyze human datasets and identify STAG3-cohesin in B-cell lymphoma cells, where STAG3 knockout strongly reduced proliferation in culture. This study therefore established STAG3 as a previously unrecognized mitotic cohesin component with roles in spermatogonial development and B-cell lymphoma proliferation, overturning the long-standing view of STAG3 as a meiosis-specific cohesin subunit. It also opened a central question for my independent program: how cell-type-specific 3D genome organization, including enhancer–promoter regulation, controls transcriptional programs and cell fate, and how its dysregulation creates context-dependent vulnerabilities in disease.

    PaperDOI: 10.1038/s41594-025-01647-w
